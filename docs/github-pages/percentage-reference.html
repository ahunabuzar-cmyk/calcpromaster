Percentage Reference — Every Formula on One Page

Almost every percentage question is one of four operations: find a percent of a number, find what percent one number is of another, find the percent change, or reverse a percentage that has already been applied. Learn those four and you can solve nearly anything without hunting for the right "type" of problem.

The one formula behind all of it

part = whole × rate, where the rate is written as a decimal (20% = 0.20). Every other formula is this one solved for a different unknown.

The four core formulas

Worked examples

  • Tip: 18% of a $64.50 bill = 64.50 × 0.18 = $11.61
  • Discount: $120 with 30% off = 120 × 0.70 = $84
  • Markup from cost: cost $40, sell at 25% markup = 40 × 1.25 = $50
  • Tax added: $200 plus 8.25% sales tax = 200 × 1.0825 = $216.50
  • Percent change: traffic 4,000 → 5,200 = 1,200/4,000 × 100 = +30%
  • Reverse: a salary after a 12% raise is 61,600 → 61,600 / 1.12 = 55,000

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Adding then subtracting the same percent. A 100 value up 20% becomes 120; 20% off 120 is 24, leaving 96, not 100. Each percent applies to a different base.
  • Percent vs percentage points. Going from 10% to 12% is a rise of 2 percentage points but a 20% increase. Headlines blur this constantly.
  • Forgetting the base. "20% cheaper" and "20% of the original" are not the same when the price already changed once.
  • Rounding too early. Keep the full value until the final step; premature rounding can shift a total by a few cents or a few units.

Percentage points vs percent

Percentage points measure the arithmetic difference between two percentages: 10% to 12% is +2 percentage points. Percent measures relative change: 10% to 12% is +20%. Use percentage points when both numbers are already percentages (interest rates, poll shares, margins), and percent when one is a base quantity.

Where percentages show up in real decisions

  • Loans: APR and interest rates move in percentage points, not percent.
  • Pay: raises, tax brackets and net pay all chain percentages off changing bases.
  • Shopping: stacked discounts are multiplied, never added — 20% + 10% is 28% off, not 30%.
  • Investing: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to break even.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for a percentage of a number? Convert the percent to a decimal and multiply: 20% of 150 = 0.20 × 150 = 30.

How do I calculate percent change? (new − old) ÷ old × 100. From 80 to 100 that is +25%.

How do I find the original number after a percentage was applied? Divide the final value by the multiplier: 68 after 15% off is 68 ÷ 0.85 = 80.

What is the difference between percent and percentage points? Percentage points are the arithmetic difference; percent is the relative change. 10% to 12% is +2 points but +20%.

Why is adding then subtracting the same percent not a no-op? Each percent applies to a different base, so 100 +20% −20% ends at 96.
