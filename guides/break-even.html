Break-Even Analysis: How Many Sales Before You Make Money?

Last updated: September 2026 · Independently QA-tested formulas

Quick answer: Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ (Price − Variable cost per unit). Every sale above that point earns profit equal to its contribution margin; every sale below it digs the hole deeper. The break-even calculator returns this instantly with charts.

Break-even is the first number every small business should know — and the one most often guessed. It answers "how much do I need to sell to stop losing money?" and, with one extension, "how much to hit my target income?"

The two cost categories (get these right first)

  • Fixed costs — the same whether you sell 10 or 10,000 units: rent, insurance, base salaries, software subscriptions, loan payments.
  • Variable costs — scale with each unit sold: materials, packaging, per-transaction payment fees, shipping, per-job labor.

Mixed costs (a phone plan with a base plus per-GB charges) split into their fixed base and variable per-unit part. Misclassifying these is the #1 source of wrong break-even numbers.

The formulas

Contribution margin (CM) = Price − Variable cost per unit
Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ CM
Break-even revenue = Fixed costs ÷ (CM ÷ Price)
Target-profit units = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ CM

Worked example: a small café

Monthly fixed costs: rent $2,400 + utilities $400 + insurance $250 + base staff $950 = $4,000. Average ticket: $5.00 coffee + pastry; variable cost per ticket (beans, milk, cup, food cost, fees): $1.50.

  1. CM = 5.00 − 1.50 = $3.50 per ticket (70%)
  2. Break-even units = 4,000 ÷ 3.50 = 1,143 tickets/month
  3. Break-even revenue = 4,000 ÷ 0.70 = $5,714/month (≈ $190/day at 30 days)

Target profit $2,000/month → (4,000 + 2,000) ÷ 3.50 = 1,715 tickets (≈ $286/day).

Margin of safety: how close to the cliff are you?

Margin of safety = (Actual sales − Break-even sales) ÷ Actual sales

If the café averages $8,000/month: (8,000 − 5,714) ÷ 8,000 = 28.6% — demand can fall by that much before losses start. Under 10% is a warning sign: one slow season or a new competitor wipes out the profit.

What the analysis teaches (levers to pull)

Raise price $0.50 (CM → $4.00)4,000 ÷ 4.00 = 1,000 tickets (−143)

Small margin improvements compound quickly — a 10% price increase typically moves break-even far more than a 10% sales increase would.

Sensitivity: what happens at other price points

Price is the strongest lever, because every extra dollar of price lands straight in contribution margin — it does not carry extra variable cost with it. Hold the café's $4,000 of fixed costs and $1.50 variable cost constant and move only the average ticket:

That is why a small, well-tested price change usually beats a large sales push: the sales route has to fund the extra beans, cups and staff hours, while the price route does not. The catch is demand — the table assumes the same number of customers keeps walking in at the higher price, which is exactly what the margin of safety tells you whether you can afford to test.

Run the same table with variable cost instead of price and the effect is smaller per dollar: on the café's $5.00 ticket, cutting variable cost by $0.50 (to $1.00) moves break-even to 1,000 tickets — the same drop a $0.50 price rise achieves, but it usually costs money or quality to get there. Improving the largest, cheapest lever is the whole discipline.

Service businesses: break-even without units

Consultants, agencies, salons and freelancers sell time, not items, so the “unit” is one billable hour (or one project, or one client on retainer — pick the one you invoice). The math is identical:

CM per hour = Billable rate − Variable cost per hour
Break-even hours = Fixed costs ÷ CM per hour

Example: a two-person studio with $6,000 of monthly fixed costs (rent $1,200, software $300, insurance $500, owner draw $4,000). The blended billable rate is $90/hour and variable cost per billed hour — contractor help, payment fees, travel — is $15, so CM is $75.

  1. Break-even = 6,000 ÷ 75 = 80 billable hours per month
  2. With two people at roughly 140 available hours each, that is 80 of 280 hours — a 29% utilisation rate
  3. At 70% utilisation (196 hours) the studio clears 196 × 75 − 6,000 = $8,700/month

Utilisation is the number that trips up service firms: a healthy-looking $90 rate hides a business that needs 80 billed hours before it earns a cent, and unbilled admin time never appears in the calculation.

What break-even does not tell you

  • Cash timing. You can be past break-even on paper and still run out of money, because invoices are paid 30–60 days later while rent and payroll leave on the first. Pair break-even with a simple cash-flow forecast.
  • Taxes. Break-even is a pre-tax operating number. Income tax and sales tax sit on top, and sales tax collected is never your revenue.
  • Capacity. A formula that says “sell 3,000 units” is useless if your kitchen, chair or van can only produce 2,000. Break-even above capacity means the model needs a price or cost change, not more effort.
  • Whether demand exists. Break-even describes the volume required, never the volume available. The market research question stays a market research question.
  • One-off costs. Equipment, build-out and deposits are not monthly fixed costs — they belong in payback period and ROI.

Break-even vs payback vs ROI

These three answer different questions and are often quoted as if they were interchangeable:

MetricQuestion it answersTime frame
Break-evenHow many units or hours until monthly losses stop?One period (month, year)
Payback periodHow long until an upfront investment is recovered?Cumulative, months or years
ROIWhat return did the money earn relative to its cost?Whole project or year

A café can break even in month three and still take two years to pay back its fit-out. Both facts are true, and a plan that only shows one of them is half a plan.

Contribution margin is the number that matters

Break-even units are fixed costs divided by the contribution margin — price minus variable cost per unit. A product priced at 40 with 25 of variable cost contributes 15, so 6,000 of fixed cost needs 400 units.

Dividing fixed costs by revenue instead is the classic error: it ignores the cost of making each unit and produces a break-even that is always too optimistic.

Fixed costs are only fixed inside a range

Rent, salaries and software are fixed within a capacity band. Add enough volume and you need another shift, another machine or another warehouse, and the fixed line steps up. Break-even computed at one capacity level does not hold at the next.

Re-run the calculation at the volume you actually expect, not the volume you hope for. The step where fixed costs jump is often closer than it looks.

Margin of safety tells you how much room there is

Margin of safety is (actual sales − break-even sales) ÷ actual sales. Ten percent means a one-tenth drop in revenue wipes out profit; forty percent means the business can absorb a bad quarter.

Track it as a trend. A shrinking margin of safety is an early signal that variable costs or discounts are eating the contribution, long before the profit line turns negative.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is break-even analysis useful for startups with no sales history?

That's exactly when it matters most — it converts a business plan into a daily sales target before money is committed. Use conservative price and cost estimates, then re-run monthly with real data.

What if I sell many products at different margins?

Use a weighted-average CM based on your sales mix, or run the analysis per product line. A gym's break-even blending $900 memberships with $40 day passes needs the mix assumption explicit.

Does break-even include owner salary?

Only if you put it in fixed costs. For a true picture, include the salary you need to survive — otherwise you've calculated break-even for the business, not for you.

What's the difference between break-even and payback period?

Break-even is per-period (usually monthly) operating coverage. Payback period asks how long an upfront investment takes to recover from cumulative cash flows — related, different question.
