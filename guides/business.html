Business Guides

Part of the CalcProMaster guides library · Updated September 2026

Business math fails most often in the same two spots: confusing margin with markup, and guessing break-even instead of computing it. These guides nail both.

Business maths rewards being explicit about which base a number is divided by: the same profit can be a 20% margin or a 25% markup, and a break-even point is only as honest as the fixed costs that went into it.

How the math works

Margin vs markup is a base confusion: margin is profit ÷ selling price, markup is profit ÷ cost. The same $10 profit on a $40-cost item selling at $50 is a 20% margin and a 25% markup — both correct, answering different questions. Pricing from a target margin needs the markup conversion: price = cost ÷ (1 − margin), so a 30% margin on $70 cost means $100, not $91.

Break-even converts that into planning: units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit). The denominator — contribution margin — is what each sale adds toward covering rent and salaries. The break-even guide runs a full café example, extends it to a target profit, and shows why a business below break-even loses money at roughly the contribution margin per unit, not at its full selling price.

One more distinction the guides keep returning to: profit is not cash. A profitable sale paid on 60-day terms still leaves payroll due on the 30th — which is why the break-even arithmetic a café needs is monthly, in cash terms, not annual. When you run the calculators, feed them the numbers from a real month (rent, average ticket, variable cost per order) rather than aspirational ones; the resulting break-even is then a line you can actually watch the business cross.

What this category covers

Break-even, margin, markup, pricing, runway, invoice factoring and unit economics. These are the numbers a small business needs weekly, and the ones most often calculated once and never updated.

Pick the cadence first: margin per sale, break-even per month, runway per quarter. A number with no review rhythm stops being a decision tool.

Fixed and variable costs behave differently

A variable cost moves with each sale and belongs in the contribution margin. A fixed cost is paid whether or not you sell anything, and belongs in the break-even denominator. Putting rent into the per-unit cost makes every unit look unprofitable at low volume.

Some costs are step-fixed: they jump at a threshold, such as a second employee or a larger warehouse. Model them as steps rather than as smooth growth.

Cash is not profit

Profit is recognised when work is done; cash arrives when the customer pays. A profitable month on 60-day terms can still miss payroll, which is why a cash-flow forecast sits beside the profit calculation.

Runway is the more useful number for a young business: months of cash at the current burn. It converts a vague worry into a deadline you can plan against.

Where these answers go wrong

  • Using revenue and cost as if they were the same base — margin divides profit by the selling price, markup divides it by the cost, so 20% margin is a 25% markup.
  • Leaving fixed costs out because they are paid monthly while the price is per unit — convert both to the same period before dividing.
  • Treating profit as cash, which is why a profitable month on 60-day invoice terms can still miss a payroll run.
  • Wanting a target margin but applying a markup percentage, which quietly underprices every sale.

Frequently asked questions

Should I price with margin or markup?

Use margin to judge a sale (profit as a share of the price you charged) and markup to price off cost (profit as a share of what you paid). Pricing from a target margin is the safer habit: price = cost ÷ (1 − margin), so a 30% margin on a $70 cost is $100, not $91.

Does break-even include tax?

Break-even as normally written covers operating costs only, so it is a pre-tax line. Profit tax is charged on profit, not on the point where profit becomes zero, so folding it into the break-even calculation would double-count it. Keep the two questions separate and apply tax afterwards.

Why does my spreadsheet disagree with a break-even calculator?

Almost always one of three inputs: rounding of contribution margin per unit, whether owner pay and loan repayments sit in fixed costs, or a different period. Put every figure on the same monthly or annual basis, list what is and is not counted, and then compare the two results.
