How Currency Conversion Works — Rates, Spreads & Hidden Fees

Money · 5 min read · Last updated September 2026

What is the mid-market rate?

The mid-market rate is the midpoint between global buy and sell prices for a currency pair. Financial data services quote it, but retail customers rarely receive it — every provider adds a margin. Any converter that uses mid-market rates, like the Currency Converter, shows you the fair reference value before provider margins.

The conversion formula

Amount in target currency = Amount × Rate
Real cost % = (Fair value − What you received) ÷ Fair value × 100

Worked example: $1,000 to euros

Mid-market rate: 1 USD = 0.92 EUR → fair value = €920

Bank offers 0.89 EUR with a $5 fixed fee → you receive €890 + lose $5 ≈ effective €885.4 equivalent.

Real cost = (920 − 885.4) ÷ 920 ≈ 3.8% — far more than the advertised "no commission".

Where the money leaks

  • Spread: the gap between the rate you buy at and the mid-market rate — often 1–4%.
  • Fixed fees: matter more on small amounts; a $5 fee is 5% of $100 but 0.5% of $1,000.
  • Weekend markups: markets close but cards keep converting, often at worse fixed rates.
  • DCC at terminals: paying in your home currency abroad usually embeds a 3–8% markup — choose local currency.
  • ATM withdrawals abroad: foreign transaction fee + local ATM operator fee can stack.

How to compare two providers fairly

  1. Compute the mid-market value of your amount.
  2. Ask each provider exactly how much target currency you'd receive, all fees included.
  3. Divide the shortfall by the fair value — the lower percentage wins.

For gold- or commodity-linked remittances, prices move with the market too — see the Gold Rate PK tool for regional pricing context.

Mid-market is the honest baseline

The mid-market rate is the midpoint between buy and sell prices on the wholesale market — the number you see on a search result. No bank or card gives you that rate, but it is the only fair yardstick for comparing what each provider actually charges.

Quote every option as a percentage away from mid-market. A provider offering "zero commission" and a rate 2.5% off mid-market is charging 2.5%, whatever the label says.

Spread, fee and markup are three different costs

The spread is the gap between the rate you are offered and mid-market. The fee is the explicit charge. The markup is a hidden adjustment baked into the rate. A cheap-looking fee often sits on top of a wide spread.

Add all three before comparing. For a transfer, the only number that matters is how much lands in the recipient's account after every charge, which is why the total-cost comparison beats any fee table.

Cash, card and transfer behave differently

A card usually gives a good rate plus a foreign-transaction fee of 1-3%, and it settles at the rate on the day the transaction clears, not the day you spent it. Cash at a bureau has a visible spread and no fee, which is often worse for small amounts and better for nothing.

Bank transfers carry a fixed fee that dominates small amounts. Below a few hundred units, the fixed fee is the whole story; above a few thousand, the spread is.

Timing and rounding

Rates move continuously, so any conversion is a snapshot. For a recurring payment, a difference of 2% in the rate is worth more than most fee negotiations, and nobody can predict it reliably — which argues for consistency rather than timing.

Rounding rules also vary: some providers round the rate to four decimals, some round the final amount. On large sums that difference is visible, so compare the final credited amount rather than the quoted rate.

Keep the receipt for each transfer. If the credited amount differs from the estimate, the receipt shows whether the gap came from the rate, from a correspondent-bank fee, or from a charge applied by the receiving bank.

FAQ

Is the rate I see on Google the one I'll get?

No — Google shows mid-market. Your provider's retail rate will be slightly worse; that difference is their revenue.

Do I lose money converting and converting back?

Yes, almost always: you pay the spread twice. Round-trip conversions typically cost several percent combined.

When is the best time to convert?

Within a day, rates move continuously and no one can reliably predict them. For large transfers, some people split the amount across several days to average the rate instead of timing one shot.

Are airport exchange counters more expensive?

Usually yes — convenience markups at airports are among the highest you'll encounter. Convert before travel or use cards where possible.
