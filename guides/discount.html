How to Calculate a Discount — Percent Off, Stacked Discounts & Real Savings

Shopping · 5 min read · Last updated September 2026

The basic discount formula

Final price = Original price × (1 − Discount% ÷ 100)
Savings = Original price − Final price

Shortcut for mental math: a 20% discount means you pay 80% of the price — move the decimal once and multiply.

Worked examples

Simple: $120 item at 25% off → 120 × 0.75 = $90 (you save $30)

Reverse: sale price $64 after 20% off → 64 ÷ 0.80 = $80 original

The stacked-discount trap

"30% + 20% off" is not 50% off. The second percentage applies after the first one is already applied:

Effective total = 1 − (1 − d₁) × (1 − d₂)

$100 at 30% off → $70. Extra 20% off applies to $70 → 70 × 0.80 = $56.

Effective discount = 44%, not 50%. The $6 difference is why stores love stacked offers.

Discount vs cashback vs coupon codes

  • Instant discount reduces the price before tax — usually the most predictable saving.
  • Cashback arrives later and may be capped or expire; treat as partial value.
  • Loyalty points have uncertain value — compare offers on final cash price, not headline percentages.

Common mistakes

  • Adding stacked percentages instead of multiplying them.
  • Applying an "extra %" to the original price instead of the reduced price.
  • Comparing percentage-off across items with very different base prices — absolute savings matter too.
  • Forgetting that tax is applied to the discounted price in most jurisdictions.

The successive-discount formula

Two stacked discounts combine by multiplication, not addition. The equivalent single discount is:

Total % off = 100 − (100 − a) × (100 − b) ÷ 100

Example: 30% plus 20% off gives 100 − (70 × 80) ÷ 100 = 100 − 56 = 44%. On a $1,000 order that is $560 to pay, not the $500 that "50% off" suggests. The same formula works for three or more discounts — keep multiplying the remaining-price factors. FAQ

How do I calculate 15% off quickly?

Take 10% (move the decimal one place left), halve it for 5%, and subtract both from the price. On $80: 10% is $8, 5% is $4, so $80 − $12 = $68.

Is a bigger percentage always better?

No — 50% off a $20 item saves $10, while 20% off a $200 item saves $40. Compare absolute savings and what you actually need.

How does buy-one-get-one compare to percent off?

BOGO on identical items equals 50% off per unit only if both items cost the same. On unequal prices it's usually worse.
