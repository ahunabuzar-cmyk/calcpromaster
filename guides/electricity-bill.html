Your Electricity Bill, Decoded: Watts × Hours × Rate

Everyday · 6 min read · Last updated September 2026

Quick answer: Every line on the bill comes from one formula: kWh = watts × hours ÷ 1,000, then cost = kWh × rate. A 300 W fridge running 8 h/day uses 72 kWh a month — 576 at 8/unit. The 1.5 kW AC at 8 h/day uses 360 kWh — 2,880. One appliance dwarfs the other; the bill follows.

The formula

kWh = watts × hours ÷ 1,000
Cost = kWh × price per unit

Worked example — a 300 W fridge compressor actually runs ~8 h/day: 300 × 8 ÷ 1,000 = 2.4 kWh/day → 72 kWh/month → 576/month at 8 per unit. A 1.5 kW AC at 8 h/day: 1.5 × 8 × 30 = 360 kWh → 2,880/month. Same formula, five-fold difference — appliance wattage × runtime is where bills are decided.

Tiered slabs change the marginal cost

Many utilities price in slabs: the first 100 kWh cheap, the next band pricier, and so on. Your average rate is not the rate for the next kWh — crossing into a higher slab makes every additional AC hour more expensive than your bill average suggests. Check your bill's slab structure before estimating savings; shaving consumption below a slab boundary saves at the higher marginal rate.

Finding the big consumers

Wattage × runtime ranks them honestly: ACs, water heaters and dryers dominate; fridges run constantly but at low power; phone chargers and idle electronics are rounding errors despite folklore. Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my electricity bill?

For each appliance: watts × daily hours ÷ 1,000 = daily kWh. Multiply by 30 for monthly kWh, sum across appliances, then multiply by your rate (respecting slab tiers if your utility uses them).

How much does it cost to run an AC?

Wattage × hours × days × rate. A 1.5 kW split AC at 8 h/day and 8/unit: 1.5 × 8 × 30 = 360 kWh → 2,880 a month. Higher rates or longer runtimes scale it linearly.

What uses the most electricity in a home?

Usually heating and cooling (AC, heaters), water heating and dryers — high wattage times long runtime. Always-on low-wattage devices like fridges matter, but chargers and idle electronics barely register.

Why is my bill higher than my kWh math?

Slab pricing (later units cost more), fixed charges, taxes and fuel-adjustment surcharges sit on top of the energy line. Check the bill’s non-energy lines before blaming the appliances.
