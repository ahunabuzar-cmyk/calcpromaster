How GPA Is Calculated (and Why Two Students With the Same Grades Can Differ)

Last updated: September 2026 · Independently QA-tested formulas

Quick answer: GPA = Σ(grade points × credit hours) ÷ Σ(credit hours). An A is worth 4.0, B 3.0, C 2.0, D 1.0, F 0. Credits are the weights — a 4-credit A moves your GPA more than a 2-credit A. Weighted scales add +0.5 (honors) or +1.0 (AP/IB) before the math.

Two students both earn A's and B's — one has a 3.8, the other a 3.4. The difference is almost never mystery; it's credits and course mix. The GPA calculator does this instantly, but the hand method takes two minutes and makes transcript checks painless.

The GPA formula

GPA = (Σ grade points × credits) ÷ (Σ credits)

Standard unweighted scale: A=4.0, A−=3.7, B+=3.3, B=3.0, B−=2.7, C+=2.3, C=2.0, D=1.0, F=0. Your school may publish slight variations — use theirs for official numbers.

Weighted vs unweighted

Weighted scales reward rigor:

  • AP / IB / college-level: A = 5.0, B = 4.0 …
  • Honors: A = 4.5, B = 3.5 …
  • Regular: A = 4.0 as before.

A student with straight A's in all-AP courses shows a 5.0 — deliberately above the unweighted ceiling. Colleges recalculate transcripts on their own scale, so a high weighted GPA is a signal, not a guarantee. Cumulative GPA across terms: sum quality points across everything, divide by total credits — never average the term GPAs directly.

What moves your GPA (and by how much)

To lift a GPA, quality points from new courses matter most. Quick math: a 13-credit semester at 3.48 (45.3 points) followed by a 4.0 13-credit semester (52 points) → cumulative 97.3 ÷ 26 = 3.74. One strong term moves it fast early on; later, big swings need big credit loads.

Common GPA mistakes

  • Averaging term GPAs instead of re-summing quality points.
  • Forgetting +/− steps — mixing A− (3.7) into an A (4.0) column drifts the result.
  • Ignoring failed repeated courses — many schools count attempts, not just the best grade; check the catalog.
  • Comparing weighted to unweighted — a 4.3 weighted ≠ 4.3 unweighted.

Cumulative GPA: re-sum, never average

The most reliable way to get your cumulative GPA is to go back to quality points. Semester 1: 15 credits at 3.40 = 51.0 points. Semester 2: 16 credits at 3.70 = 59.2 points. Cumulative = (51.0 + 59.2) ÷ (15 + 16) = 110.2 ÷ 31 = 3.55. Notice that averaging the two term GPAs (3.40 + 3.70) ÷ 2 = 3.55 happens to match here only because the credit counts are nearly equal — with a 12-credit and an 18-credit term the shortcut breaks. The GPA Calculator re-sums quality points for you across any number of terms.

Also keep in mind that grading scales differ between institutions: some add 0.3 for a plus and subtract 0.3 for a minus, others cap A+ at 4.0, and a few use a 4.33 or 5.0 ceiling. Always recalculate on your school's published scale before comparing yourself to any external cutoff.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does one B hurt a 4.0?

In a 15-credit semester: (60 − 15) ÷ 15 → one 3-credit B lands you at 57 ÷ 15 = 3.8. Early semesters hurt more precisely because the credit base is small.

Is a 3.5 GPA good for scholarships?

Many merit awards set 3.0–3.5 as thresholds; competitive awards often look at 3.7+. Pair the number with the school's own scale — some publish exactly what they recalculate.

How do I convert percentage to GPA?

There is no universal formula; common US approximations map 90–100% → 4.0, 80–89% → 3.0–3.9 by band. International applicants should use WES-style evaluations rather than DIY tables.

What GPA do I need for grad school?

Common guidance: 3.0 is a floor for many programs, 3.5+ for competitive ones — with research, essays and test scores carrying real weight beyond the number.
