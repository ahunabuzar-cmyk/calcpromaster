Health & Fitness Guides

Part of the CalcProMaster guides library · Updated September 2026

Health metrics are only useful when you know what they measure, what they ignore, and when they don't apply. These guides cover both the formula and its limits.

Health calculators convert measurements into population estimates, and the population is the point: each formula is a best fit for a group, not a measurement of you.

How the math works

The two formulas here are simple; the honest part is what they assume. BMI = weight ÷ height² — a population-screening ratio that cannot distinguish muscle from fat. TDEE = BMR × activity multiplier — and the multiplier (1.2 sedentary to 1.9 very active) is the least precise number in the whole chain: two people with identical stats can differ by several hundred calories in reality. The calories guide shows how to pick a multiplier honestly and adjust from observed results instead of trusting the first estimate.

Every figure in this section is a reference, not a prescription. Formulas like Mifflin-St Jeor are population regressions — they're right on average and uncertain for any individual. That's why each guide ends by naming what the metric cannot see, and why the blog's BMI explainer pairs the formula with companion measurements rather than treating one number as a verdict.

For anyone changing weight or fitness, the working method is: compute a starting estimate, hold it for two to three weeks while recording actual outcomes, then adjust by observed reality — roughly 100–200 kcal at a time. That loop (estimate → observe → adjust) is more reliable than any formula refinement, because it uses your body's actual response instead of a population average. The calculators give you the honest starting point; the record-keeping makes it yours.

What this category covers

BMI, body fat, BMR, calorie needs, protein targets, heart-rate zones and hydration. The formulas are well known; what they share is a wide margin of individual error, because they infer a whole body from a few measurements.

Use them to establish a starting point and to track direction. A single reading is a data point, not a verdict.

Screening tools versus diagnostic tools

BMI was designed to describe populations, not to diagnose individuals. It misses muscle mass, fat distribution and ethnicity-specific risk thresholds, all of which change the interpretation.

If a number on this site worries you, the next step is a measurement with a clinician, not a stricter calculation. These tools organise information; they do not replace assessment.

Consistency beats precision

Weigh yourself at the same time of day, under the same conditions, and look at the weekly average. Day-to-day changes are mostly water and food, and reacting to them produces decisions that make no sense over a month.

The same applies to body fat and heart rate: repeatable conditions matter more than the absolute accuracy of the method.

Where these answers go wrong

  • Reading BMI as an individual diagnosis when it was calibrated on populations and misreads muscular builds.
  • Applying an activity multiplier to a metabolic rate and then eating back exercise calories a second time.
  • Building heart-rate zones on the age estimate when a measured maximum is available.
  • Weighing daily and reacting to water swings, which move far faster than fat does.

Frequently asked questions

Should I trust BMI or body fat?

They answer different questions. BMI compares weight with height across a population, which is useful for screening and hopeless for a muscular individual. A body-fat estimate or waist measurement says more about an individual. Read BMI as a first filter, then use a measure that accounts for composition.

Which metabolic-rate formula should I pick?

Mifflin-St Jeor is the most widely used modern estimate and is usually closer than the older Harris-Benedict figures for most adults. Both are population fits with a typical error of a few percent either way, so the honest approach is to start from the estimate and adjust it against what your weight actually does over three to four weeks.

How accurate are calorie and zone estimates?

Good enough to plan with, not good enough to treat as exact. Metabolic estimates can be off by a few hundred calories a day between individuals, and the common "220 minus age" heart-rate rule can miss by ten to fifteen beats. Zones built on a measured maximum, or on heart-rate reserve, are noticeably better.
