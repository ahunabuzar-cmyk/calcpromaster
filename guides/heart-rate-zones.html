Heart Rate Zones: Math for Training, With the Error Bars Shown

Health · 6 min read · Last updated September 2026

Quick answer: Training zones are percentages of a reference heart rate. The classic estimate HRmax = 220 − age carries a standard deviation of ~10–12 bpm; the Tanaka formula (208 − 0.7×age) fits measured maxima better in adults. A 35-year-old with Tanaka max ≈ 183.5 bpm trains zone 2 (60–70%) at roughly 110–128 bpm.

Step 1: estimate your maximum heart rate

Classic (Fox): HRmax = 220 − age
Tanaka: HRmax = 208 − 0.7 × age

Age 35: Fox gives 185, Tanaka gives 183.5 — close here. At age 60 they diverge: Fox 160, Tanaka 166. Fox's equation came from a scatter of studies and systematically underestimates HRmax in older adults; Tanaka was fitted on a larger measured sample. Neither beats an actual field test (a hard, properly warmed-up effort) or a lab test.

Step 2: cut the range into five zones

Using Tanaka 183.5 bpm for our 35-year-old (reserve method shown because it anchors zones to resting heart rate too: HRR = HRmax − HRrest; zones = HRrest + %×HRR, with HRrest = 60): HRR = 123.5.

Z1 recovery50–60%Easy, conversational92–110

Z5 VO₂max90–100%All-out, minutes at most165–183

Most endurance training time belongs in Z1–Z2 — the "80/20" distribution popularized by polarized-training research. Zone 2 mislabeled as Z3 is the most common self-coaching error, and it usually comes from an HRmax formula that underestimates your true max.

Limitations: a formula's ±10–12 bpm error can shift you a full zone. Medications (notably beta-blockers), heat, caffeine, stress and dehydration all move heart rate independently of effort. Calibrate zones against perceived effort and — if possible — a measured field test. Heart conditions need clinical guidance first.

The age formula is a population average

The 220 minus age estimate of maximum heart rate has a standard deviation of roughly 10-12 beats per minute. For a 40-year-old, the plausible maximum spans something like 160 to 200, which moves every zone derived from it.

A measured maximum from a field test or a lab is far better if you train seriously. The formula is a placeholder, and zones built on it inherit its error.

Zones as percentages of maximum

Common bands run from 50-60% for recovery, 60-70% for aerobic base, 70-80% for tempo and 80-90%+ for threshold and VO2 work. The boundaries are conventions, not sharp physiological switches.

Heart rate also drifts upward during a long effort at constant pace, from heat and dehydration. Judging effort by pace or power as well as heart rate prevents chasing a number that is moving for reasons unrelated to intensity.

Medication and individual variation

Beta blockers and some other medications lower heart rate directly, which makes any age-based zone wrong. So does a naturally high or low resting rate.

Use perceived effort as a cross-check, and treat chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness as a reason to stop rather than a zone to push through.

Frequently asked questions

Which max heart rate formula should I use?

Tanaka (208 − 0.7×age) tracks measured maxima better than the classic 220 − age, especially over 40. Both carry roughly ±10–12 bpm error — treat the output as a starting band, then adjust with a field test or how effort actually feels.

What is zone 2 and why is everyone talking about it?

Zone 2 is the intensity where you can hold a conversation with slightly longer breaths — roughly 60–70% of max heart rate. It builds aerobic base with low stress and is where most weekly endurance volume is best spent.

Do beta-blockers change heart rate zones?

They lower both resting and maximum heart rate substantially, so percentage-of-max formulas computed from age alone will mislead. Anyone on such medication should set zones with their doctor or use perceived-exertion scales instead.

Is a higher resting heart rate bad?

For adults, 60–100 bpm is the usual reference band, with well-trained people often in the 40s–50s. A sudden sustained rise from your own normal can signal illness, overtraining or dehydration — it is a trend worth watching, not a single reading.
