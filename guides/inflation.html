How Inflation Is Calculated — CPI, Purchasing Power & Real Values

Economics · 5 min read · Last updated September 2026

What inflation actually measures

Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices rises over time. Statistical agencies (the BLS in the US, PBS in Pakistan, ONS in the UK) track the price of a fixed "basket" of everyday goods and services and publish it as an index — the best known is the Consumer Price Index (CPI).

The inflation rate formula

Inflation % = (Index_now − Index_before) ÷ Index_before × 100

Converting money across years

Value in year N = Amount × (1 + rate)^N
Real value = Nominal value ÷ (1 + rate)^N

Worked example: what $100 becomes

$100 held for 10 years at 3% average inflation:

100 × 1.03¹⁰ ≈ $134.39 needed then to buy what $100 buys today.

Equivalently, today's $100 will have purchasing power of 100 ÷ 1.03¹⁰ ≈ $74.41 in future terms.

At 6% inflation the same figure drops to ≈ $55.84 — the gap between 3% and 6% compounds dramatically.

Why it matters for every calculation

  • Salaries: a 4% raise with 6% inflation is a real pay cut.
  • Investments: always compare returns against inflation — 8% return at 6% inflation is a 2% real gain.
  • Long-term goals: a retirement target set in today's money must be inflated to the target year.
  • Loans: fixed payments get cheaper in real terms as inflation rises — good for borrowers with fixed rates.

Common mistakes

  • Using headline CPI for personal costs — your personal basket (rent, food, fuel) may inflate faster.
  • Adding inflation to returns instead of compounding: (1 + r) ÷ (1 + i) − 1 is the exact real return.
  • Ignoring currency-specific inflation — global averages don't apply to local prices.

Note: Official CPI methodology is periodically revised (basket weights, quality adjustments). This guide explains the mechanics; for official statistics use your national statistics agency's publications.

FAQ

What is the difference between CPI and core inflation?

Core inflation excludes volatile food and energy prices to reveal the underlying trend; headline CPI includes everything consumers buy.

Can inflation be negative?

Yes — falling prices is called deflation. It sounds beneficial but often signals weak demand and can delay purchases, hurting the economy.

How does inflation affect compound interest earnings?

It reduces the real return. A 7% nominal return during 5% inflation grows purchasing power by only about 1.9% per year, not 7%.
