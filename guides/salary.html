Gross Salary vs In-Hand Salary — How to Calculate Take-Home Pay

Career & Money · 6 min read · Last updated September 2026

Gross vs net vs CTC

  • Gross salary — total before any deductions (basic + allowances + bonuses).
  • Net / in-hand salary — what actually reaches your bank account after tax and deductions.
  • CTC (Cost to Company) — everything the employer spends on you, including contributions you never see in-hand (retirement funds, insurance premiums, gratuity provisions).

Key insight: CTC ≠ gross ≠ net. The gap between the offer letter and the bank credit confuses almost everyone at first.

The gross-to-net formula

In-hand = Gross − Income tax − Retirement contribution − Insurance − Other deductions

Step-by-step: estimate your in-hand salary

  1. Start from gross annual salary (or CTC minus employer-only contributions).
  2. Subtract pre-tax contributions — retirement fund (provident fund / 401(k) / NPS), health insurance where pre-tax.
  3. Compute income tax on the remaining taxable income using your country's brackets (see our income tax guide).
  4. Subtract post-tax deductions — professional tax, payroll-assigned loan EMIs, garnishments.
  5. Divide by pay periods — 12 for monthly, 24/26 for semi-monthly or biweekly schedules.

Worked example

CTC $30,000 includes $2,400 employer retirement contribution → gross = $27,600

Own retirement contribution 5% of gross = $1,380 → taxable income = $26,220

Illustrative tax on $26,220 ≈ $1,900

In-hand = 27,600 − 1,380 − 1,900 = $24,320/year ≈ $2,027/month

Common mistakes

  • Comparing offers on CTC without normalizing employer-side contributions.
  • Assuming a 10% raise means 10% more in-hand — progressive tax shrinks the net gain.
  • Forgetting one-time components (joining bonus) that inflate year-1 CTC.

What a raise actually does to in-hand pay

Raises are advertised in gross percentages but experienced in net rupees/dollars, and progressive tax shrinks the gap. Take a $27,600 gross (the worked example above) receiving a 10% raise to $30,360, with the same illustrative tax function: tax on $26,220 was ≈ $1,900; tax on the raised taxable income (30,360 − 1,518 contribution = $28,842) rises to roughly $2,300. In-hand moves from $24,320 to about $26,542 — a 9.1% net gain on a 10% gross raise. Still good — but the marginal slice of the raise was taxed at the top rate, not the average one.

This is also why offer comparisons mislead: a higher CTC with a larger employer-only contribution can leave less in-hand than a smaller offer structured differently. The normalized comparison is always monthly in-hand under identical assumptions — the salary calculator computes gross-to-net for your inputs, and the tax guide explains why the marginal slice hurts.

Regional note for the three audiences this site serves most: in India, the new regime's ₹75,000 standard deduction and slab widths mean salaried net pay is now much closer to gross × 0.9 at median salaries than the old-regime folklore suggests; in Pakistan, salaried schedules differ from non-salaried rates and annual tax credits change the net materially; in the UAE, there is no personal income tax — the gross-to-net gap comes from end-of-service provisions and voluntary contributions, not withholding. The mechanics stay the same; the deduction lines change.

Note: Salary structures, contribution rates and tax rules differ by country and employer. This guide explains the mechanics; use your payslip's actual deduction lines for precise figures.

Gross, net and the gap between them

The gap between gross salary and take-home is made of income tax, social insurance, retirement contributions and any employer-specific deductions. The size of that gap varies enormously between jurisdictions and between income levels.

Compare offers on net where you will actually live and work. A higher gross in a higher-tax location can leave less in your account, and the difference is often larger than the salary gap that prompted the comparison.

Allowances and benefits have different tax treatment

Housing, transport and meal allowances are taxed differently across jurisdictions, and some are exempt up to a limit. A package with a large allowance can be worth more than the headline figure suggests, or less if it is fully taxable.

Ask for the breakdown in writing before comparing. The structure of a package is part of its value, not a detail to settle after accepting.

Overtime, bonus and variable pay

Bonuses are often taxed at a higher withholding rate and reconciled later, which makes the first payslip after a bonus look punitive. Overtime rules depend on employment status and local law, not on the contract's wording alone.

Plan on base salary and treat variable pay as upside. A budget that requires the bonus to work is a budget with no margin.

FAQ

Why is my first month's salary different?

Payroll often runs on a lag: your first credit may be a partial month or a shifted period. Deduction start dates can also differ from your joining date.

How do I compare two offers fairly?

Normalize both to monthly in-hand using the same assumptions (retirement contribution %, tax regime), then compare non-cash benefits separately.

Do bonuses get taxed differently?

They are income taxed at your marginal rate in most systems; some countries apply supplemental withholding at payout but true it up at filing.
