Science Guides

Part of the CalcProMaster guides library · Physics & chemistry fundamentals

Science calculators look intimidating because textbooks bury the formula under notation. Each of these explainers strips a physical law down to its variables, shows a worked number, and tells you which units must match before the answer means anything.

Methodology: SI-based physical constants and standard scientific formulas, with units carried through the working.

Scientific calculators are only as good as their units: the gas laws want kelvin and pascals, mole calculations want a matching molar mass, and most formulas carry conditions that are easy to violate by accident.

How the math works

Start every problem by writing the formula, then listing each variable with its unit. Density = mass ÷ volume: 27 g of aluminium occupying 10 cm³ gives 2.7 g/cm³ — the accepted value, which is how you know the units were right. Enter that mass in kilograms while volume stays in cm³ and the result is 0.0027, silently meaningless.

Two habits prevent most wrong answers. First, convert everything to the formula’s base units before calculating — the unit conversion guide lists the exact factors. Second, sanity-check the magnitude: a human body is roughly 1,000 kg/m³ (we barely float), the Earth about 5,500 kg/m³, sea-level air about 1.2 kg/m³. Results far outside familiar ranges usually mean a unit slipped, not that you discovered new physics.

Why do my science calculator results look wrong?

Almost always a unit mismatch: grams fed into a kg-based formula, or kPa treated as Pa. Convert every input to the formula’s base units first — the unit conversion guide shows the exact factors.

Which base units does SI use?

Metre (length), kilogram (mass), second (time), ampere (current), kelvin (temperature), mole (amount), and candela (luminous intensity). Every derived unit — newtons, joules, pascals — is built from these.

What this category covers

Physics and chemistry calculations: motion, energy, concentration, molarity, gas laws and half-life. The formulas are exact; the answers depend entirely on consistent units and on whether the model applies to the situation.

Use them for coursework and for quick estimates, and check the assumptions in the guide before trusting a number in a real system.

Significant figures and rounding

A result cannot be more precise than its inputs. Reporting 4.7382 from inputs given to two significant figures is a claim about measurement precision that the data does not support.

Carry full precision through the calculation and round only the final answer, to the precision of the least precise input. That is the convention most exam boards and journals expect.

When the model stops applying

Ideal gas behaviour fails at high pressure and low temperature. Simple projectile motion ignores drag, which matters beyond short ranges. Half-life assumes first-order kinetics, which not every reaction follows.

Knowing the boundary of a model is part of using it. An answer outside the model is not wrong arithmetic; it is the wrong tool.

Where these answers go wrong

  • Entering temperatures in Celsius where the equation requires kelvin — every gas-law style formula needs absolute temperature.
  • Pairing grams with a molar mass and then reporting the result as if it were a concentration per litre.
  • Rounding at each intermediate step, so error accumulates before the final significant figures are chosen.
  • Applying a formula outside the range it was derived for, such as an ideal-gas estimate near condensation.

Frequently asked questions

How many significant figures should I report?

As many as the least precise input supports. If one measurement is 2.1 (two figures) and another is 12.35, the answer carries two. Round only at the end — rounding in the middle is what makes a hand calculation disagree with the calculator by more than the measurement error.

Why does temperature have to be in kelvin?

Because the gas laws are proportional to absolute temperature: halving the kelvin value halves the volume at constant pressure, which is only true on a scale that starts at absolute zero. Celsius has an arbitrary zero, so using it produces answers that are wrong by a fixed offset.

When do the standard approximations stop working?

Each formula has conditions printed with it for a reason. Ideal-gas estimates drift as pressure rises or as a gas approaches condensation; small-angle approximations fail past roughly fifteen degrees; frictionless and massless assumptions fail as soon as real hardware is involved. Check the condition before trusting the number.
