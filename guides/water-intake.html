Daily Water Intake: The Formula, the Myth, and Your Sweat

Health · 5 min read · Last updated September 2026

Quick answer: A reasonable weight-based baseline is 30–35 ml per kg of body weight per day, plus roughly 400–600 ml per hour of exercise, more in heat. A 70 kg adult lands around 2.1–2.5 L/day from all fluids and food — not the mythical eight 8-oz glasses by decree, but not far off either.

The weight-based formula, worked

Baseline (ml/day) = body weight (kg) × 33 (midpoint of 30–35)
+ exercise: ~500 ml per hour of activity
+ heat: +250–500 ml on hot days

Example — 70 kg office worker with one hour of gym: 70 × 33 = 2,310 ml, plus ~500 ml for the workout = ~2.8 L/day. About 20–30% of fluid intake normally comes from food, so drinking ~2.2 L covers it. The calculator does this arithmetic with your own numbers.

Where "8 glasses" came from — and why it stuck

The 1945 US Food and Nutrition Board recommendation said roughly 1 ml per kcal of food energy — about 2–2.5 L — but the accompanying sentence noting that much of it is in food got dropped in repetition. Eight 8-oz glasses (1.9 L) is a memorable rounding of a rough 1940s figure, not a measured human requirement. Actual needs vary roughly 1.5–4 L/day between people.

Where the eight-glasses figure comes from

The eight-glasses rule is a rough population heuristic, not a physiological target. Fluid needs vary with body mass, activity, climate and diet, and roughly a fifth of daily fluid normally comes from food.

The widely used reference intakes are around 2.7 litres a day for women and 3.7 for men in temperate conditions, including fluid from food and other drinks. That is a total, not an instruction to drink that much water.

Thirst, urine colour and exercise

Thirst is a reliable signal for healthy adults at rest, though it lags during hard exercise and in heat. Pale straw-coloured urine is a reasonable practical check; completely clear urine usually means you are drinking more than you need.

For exercise, add roughly 400-800 ml per hour of moderate activity, more in heat, and include electrolytes when sessions run past an hour or sweating is heavy.

When more water is not better

Drinking far beyond need can dilute blood sodium, which is dangerous and has caused deaths in endurance events and water-drinking contests. The condition is rare but the direction of the risk matters: more is not automatically safer.

People with heart, kidney or liver conditions are commonly given fluid restrictions, and a generic calculator is the wrong tool for them.

Frequently asked questions

Is 8 glasses of water a day actually right?

It is a reasonable rough total for many mid-size adults but was never a measured requirement. Weight-based estimates (30–35 ml/kg), activity, climate and food-derived fluid give a more personal number.

Do tea and coffee count?

Yes. Despite folklore, moderate caffeine does not dehydrate regular consumers, and all beverages plus water-rich food contribute to daily fluid intake.

Can I drink too much water?

Yes — drinking liters beyond thirst in a short window can dilute blood sodium (hyponatremia), which is dangerous. Endurance athletes drinking large volumes over hours are the classic risk group; electrolyte replacement matters there.

How do I know if I am drinking enough?

For healthy adults, pale-straw-colored urine and drinking to thirst are the practical signals. Dark urine suggests you could use more; completely clear all day suggests you may be overdoing it.
